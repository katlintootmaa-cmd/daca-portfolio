# Hiring Guide for the Role: Marketing Analyst

Estonian version: [README.md](README.md)

## Role Focus

A Marketing Analyst helps UrbanStyle understand campaign performance, customer segments, channel profitability, and the impact of marketing on sales. The role combines data analysis and marketing logic.

## How to Use This Guide

This guide can be used throughout the Marketing Analyst hiring process: writing the job posting, reviewing CV and LinkedIn, evaluating the campaign portfolio, preparing a practical case, conducting the interview, and making the final decision.

For each candidate, three main questions should be evaluated:

- whether the candidate can connect campaign data with sales and profitability;
- whether they understand marketing metrics such as conversion, ROI, CAC, and retention;
- whether they can give practical recommendations to the marketing team based on data.

Recommended decision:

- **Move to the next round** if the candidate can analyze campaign impact, separates visibility metrics from business impact, and offers testable next steps.
- **Needs additional checking** if the candidate knows the metrics but does not connect them enough with sales or customer segments.
- **Not suitable at the moment** if the candidate talks only about clicks and views and cannot evaluate the real profitability of a campaign.

- Role A, the hiring manager, evaluates whether the candidate's CV, LinkedIn, and portfolio show campaign analysis, channel understanding, and interpretation of marketing results.
- Role B, the technical interviewer, evaluates whether the candidate can work with campaign data, calculate metrics, and use SQL or Python to answer marketing questions.
- Role C, the HR manager, evaluates whether the candidate can collaborate with the marketing team, explain results to a creative team, and offer practical ideas.
- Role D, the team lead, evaluates whether the candidate can prioritize campaigns, think experimentally, and connect analysis with business impact.

## Role View and Work

In this group work, we looked at the Marketing Analyst role from four perspectives. The hiring manager evaluated how CV, LinkedIn, and the job posting highlight marketing analytics experience. The technical interviewer focused on campaign data, SQL, dashboards, conversion, and ROI calculation. The HR manager evaluated whether the candidate can communicate with the marketing team and explain results clearly. The team lead looked at whether the analyst's recommendations are practical and help the marketing and sales teams make decisions.

We used the same four views in every chapter. This connected marketing business logic, data skills, collaboration, and presentation of results.

## 1. Technical Skills Evaluation

In a Marketing Analyst role, the candidate is expected to analyze campaigns, conversion, customer segments, ROI, and sales channels. SQL and visualization are important, and Python can be an advantage.

The employer checks whether the candidate can explain which campaign worked and why.

Group result: the technical test could be a campaign data analysis where the candidate calculates conversion and campaign profitability.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the candidate correctly calculates conversion, ROI/CAC, compares channels, and separates clicks from real business impact.
- Asked: "Which campaign was most profitable and why?", "How would you measure the impact of a campaign on sales?", "What data would you need in addition to click statistics?"

## 2. Portfolio Evaluation

A Marketing Analyst portfolio should include a project about customer segmentation, campaign analysis, or sales channel comparison.

The employer checks whether the candidate can give marketing recommendations based on data, not only report results.

Group result: a strong portfolio connects campaign, customer, and sales result.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the project has a campaign goal, target group, metrics, interpretation of results, and practical marketing recommendations.
- Asked: "Which target group did you analyze?", "How did you reach the recommendation?", "What would you change in the next campaign based on this data?"

## 3. CV and LinkedIn Evaluation

The CV should emphasize marketing metrics: conversion rate, ROI, CAC, retention, customer segments, and campaign results. Previous marketing experience is a major advantage.

The LinkedIn headline could be: "Marketing Analyst | SQL, Campaign Analytics, Customer Segmentation | Data-Driven Growth".

Group result: the profile must show that the candidate understands both data and marketing goals.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the CV includes marketing metrics, campaign results, channel comparison, segment analysis, and tools used.
- Asked: "Which marketing metric is most important to you and why?", "How does your previous marketing experience help you as an analyst?", "Give an example of a data-driven marketing decision."

## 4. 30-60-90 Day Onboarding Plan

First 30 days: learning marketing channels, campaigns, and metrics.

Days 30-60: analyzing the results of one campaign and improving the dashboard.

Days 60-90: analyzing the profitability of one customer segment or channel with recommendations.

Group result: a Marketing Analyst must quickly learn how marketing activity affects sales.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the plan connects campaigns, channels, customer segments, and sales results with increasingly independent analysis.
- Asked: "Which campaigns would you study first?", "How would you agree on success metrics with the marketing team?", "Which channel's profitability would you analyze within 60 days?"

## 5. Writing a Job Offer

The job posting should emphasize campaign analysis, customer segmentation, ROI measurement, and collaboration with marketing and sales teams.

Requirements: SQL, Excel/Sheets, visualization, understanding of marketing metrics, and good communication skills.

Group result: the posting must fit both a candidate with a data background and a career changer with a marketing background.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the job posting explains campaign analysis, customer segments, ROI, channel comparison, and collaboration with the marketing team.
- Asked: "Is the posting too technical or too general?", "Are marketing metrics clearly named?", "Is the portfolio opportunity mentioned?"

## 6. Interview Scenario

The interview could include a case: "A campaign brought many clicks, but sales did not grow. How would you investigate the reason?"

The candidate should think about channels, conversion, target audience, offer, and the purchase journey.

Group result: the Marketing Analyst interview must evaluate campaign logic, data skills, and business recommendations.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the candidate investigates the full purchase journey, not only campaign clicks, and offers testable next steps.
- Asked: "There are many clicks but few sales. What could be the reason?", "How do you check whether the problem is the target group or the offer?", "What recommendation would you give to the marketing manager?"

**More Detailed Interview Structure**

- 5 minutes: introduction and listening to the candidate's background.
- 15 minutes: portfolio or campaign analysis walk-through where the candidate explains the goal, metrics, and recommendation.
- 15 minutes: practical case, for example a campaign brings many clicks but sales do not grow.
- 15 minutes: STAR method questions about collaboration with marketing, sales, and management.
- 5 minutes: candidate questions.
- 5 minutes: explaining next steps.

**Interview Evaluation Criteria**

- Technical skill: SQL, campaign data, conversion, ROI/CAC, and visualization.
- Business thinking: whether the candidate separates visibility metrics from real sales or profit impact.
- Communication: whether they can turn the result into a practical recommendation for the marketing team.
- Collaboration and learning ability: whether they can work with marketing and sales and ask the right clarifying questions.

## Strong vs Weak Candidate

## Evaluation Scale Summary

| Area | 1 - needs development | 2 - suitable level | 3 - strong level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metrics | Knows only general visibility metrics | Calculates conversion, ROI, or CAC | Connects metrics with sales, profit, and segment |
| Analysis | Describes results superficially | Compares campaigns and channels | Finds causes and checks hypotheses |
| Recommendations | Does not offer action | Gives one practical recommendation | Offers prioritized next steps |
| Collaboration | Does not consider marketing team needs | Explains results understandably | Translates analysis into a campaign plan |

The candidate is a strong recommendation if metrics and recommendations are at least level 2 and the analysis reaches business impact.

**Strong candidate:** understands the connection between campaigns, customer segments, and sales results. They can calculate conversion, ROI, or CAC and explain which channel or target group creates real value. The portfolio includes, for example, campaign analysis or customer segmentation with a practical marketing recommendation.

**Weak candidate:** focuses only on clicks, views, or general marketing talk, but does not connect results with sales or business impact. They cannot distinguish campaign visibility from real profitability, and their CV or LinkedIn does not include concrete marketing metrics, results, or tools.
