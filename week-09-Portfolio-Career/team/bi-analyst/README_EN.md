# Hiring Guide for the Role: BI Analyst

Estonian version: [README.md](README.md)

## Role Focus

A BI Analyst focuses on dashboards, reports, and making management information accessible. At UrbanStyle, a BI Analyst would help create views for sales, customers, products, inventory, and campaign results.

## How to Use This Guide

This guide can be used throughout the BI Analyst hiring process: writing the job posting, reviewing CV and LinkedIn, evaluating the portfolio, preparing a dashboard task, conducting the interview, and making the final hiring decision.

For each candidate, three main questions should be evaluated:

- whether the candidate can create clear and useful dashboards;
- whether they understand KPIs, filters, and data model logic;
- whether they can explain BI reports to business users.

Recommended decision:

- **Move to the next round** if the candidate can build a readable dashboard, explain KPI choices, and check data reliability.
- **Needs additional checking** if the candidate can make visuals, but the KPI logic or usability remains weak.
- **Not suitable at the moment** if the candidate creates charts without understanding what decision the report supports.

## Role View and Work

In this group work, we looked at the BI Analyst role from four perspectives. The hiring manager focused on how BI experience appears in CV, LinkedIn, and the job posting. The technical interviewer focused on SQL, data models, KPI logic, and dashboard quality. The HR manager looked at communication with business users. The team lead considered everyday BI work: report ownership, documentation, feedback, and usability.

We used the same four views in every chapter so that the guide would evaluate both technical BI ability and the ability to support decision-making.

## 1. Technical Skills Evaluation

In a BI Analyst role, SQL, understanding of data models, and visualization tools are expected. The candidate should be able to create metrics, filters, charts, and a dashboard that supports decisions.

The employer checks whether the candidate understands KPIs and can avoid confusing or misleading visualizations.

Group result: the technical task could be creating a dashboard from UrbanStyle sales and product data, with KPIs, filters, and a short interpretation.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether KPI definitions are correct, filters work, charts are readable, and the report answers a business question.
- Asked: "Why did you choose these KPIs?", "How would you check that the dashboard numbers are correct?", "Who is the user of this dashboard?"

## 2. Portfolio Evaluation

A BI Analyst portfolio should include dashboards or reports where the target user, KPIs, data source, and business conclusion are clearly explained.

The employer checks whether the candidate can create a dashboard that is not only visually nice, but usable for a specific decision.

Group result: a strong BI portfolio includes a Power BI, Tableau, Plotly, or Streamlit dashboard with a clear README.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the dashboard has a clear audience, KPI definitions, filters, readable layout, and business interpretation.
- Asked: "What decision does this dashboard support?", "Which metric was hardest to define?", "How would you improve the dashboard after user feedback?"

## 3. CV and LinkedIn Evaluation

The CV should highlight dashboard tools, SQL, KPI work, reporting, and business-facing outputs. It is stronger when the candidate names concrete dashboards and their purpose.

The LinkedIn headline could be: "BI Analyst | SQL, Power BI, KPI Dashboards | Turning Data into Management Insight".

Group result: CV, LinkedIn, and portfolio should show that the candidate can build reliable reporting for business users.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the CV includes BI tools, SQL, KPI examples, report users, and measurable outcomes.
- Asked: "Which dashboard are you most proud of?", "How did users use your report?", "How do you keep a dashboard maintainable?"

## 4. 30-60-90 Day Onboarding Plan

First 30 days: learn UrbanStyle's KPI definitions, data sources, existing reports, and main users.

Days 30-60: improve one existing report or create a small dashboard with stakeholder feedback.

Days 60-90: create one complete BI report, such as a sales or inventory dashboard, with documentation and user guidance.

Group result: onboarding should move from understanding existing reports to creating a reliable and usable BI solution.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the plan includes KPI learning, data validation, user feedback, and a concrete dashboard output.
- Asked: "Which report would you review first?", "How would you gather user needs?", "How would you document metric definitions?"

## 5. Writing a Job Offer

The job posting should emphasize dashboard development, KPI reporting, SQL, data model understanding, and collaboration with business users.

Requirements: SQL, Power BI or similar BI tool, Excel, visualization, KPI thinking, and clear communication.

Group result: the posting should invite candidates who can create reliable and decision-supporting reports, not only nice charts.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the posting describes real BI tasks, required tools, KPI ownership, and collaboration with users.
- Asked: "Are the requirements realistic for a junior role?", "Does the posting explain what reports are needed?", "Is portfolio evidence encouraged?"

## 6. Interview Scenario

The interview could include a case: "Management needs a sales dashboard, but the numbers from finance and marketing do not match. How would you investigate and build a trusted report?"

The candidate should think about metric definitions, data sources, filters, validation, and user needs.

Group result: the BI Analyst interview must evaluate dashboard thinking, KPI logic, data validation, and communication.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the candidate clarifies the user, checks metric definitions, validates numbers, and chooses suitable visuals.
- Asked: "How would you define revenue?", "How do you avoid double-counting?", "How would you explain a dashboard to a non-technical manager?"

**More Detailed Interview Structure**

- 5 minutes: introduction and candidate background.
- 15 minutes: dashboard portfolio walk-through.
- 15 minutes: practical BI case with KPI and dashboard design.
- 15 minutes: STAR questions about feedback, deadlines, and communicating with stakeholders.
- 5 minutes: candidate questions.
- 5 minutes: next steps.

**Interview Evaluation Criteria**

- Technical skill: SQL, BI tool logic, data models, KPI calculations, and validation.
- Business thinking: whether the candidate connects reports with decisions.
- Communication: whether they can explain dashboard choices clearly.
- Collaboration and learning ability: whether they gather feedback and improve the solution.

## Strong vs Weak Candidate

## Evaluation Scale Summary

| Area | 1 - needs development | 2 - suitable level | 3 - strong level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Technical skill | Does not understand data model or BI tool logic | Creates a simple dashboard | Creates a reliable and maintainable report |
| KPI thinking | Chooses metrics randomly | Connects KPIs with a decision | Justifies metrics and limitations |
| Usability | Dashboard is confusing | Dashboard is readable and has filters | Dashboard fits a specific user |
| Communication | Cannot explain choices | Explains the report understandably | Collects feedback and improves the solution |

The candidate is a strong recommendation if they reach at least level 2 in every area and KPI thinking or usability is at level 3.

**Strong candidate:** can create dashboards that are clear, usable, and connected to management decisions. They justify KPI choices, check data reliability, and think about user needs. Their portfolio includes a Power BI, Tableau, Plotly, or Streamlit dashboard whose README explains the audience, metrics, and decisions the dashboard supports.

**Weak candidate:** creates charts without a clear business question, does not define KPIs, and does not check whether numbers are reliable. The dashboard may look nice but is hard to use, and the candidate cannot explain how it supports a decision.
