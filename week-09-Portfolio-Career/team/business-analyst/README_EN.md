# Hiring Guide for the Role: Business Analyst

Estonian version: [README.md](README.md)

## Role Focus

A Business Analyst helps UrbanStyle understand business needs, formulate problems, and translate them into analysis tasks. The role is less purely technical than Data Analyst, but requires very strong business thinking, communication, and the ability to support decisions with data.

## How to Use This Guide

This guide can be used throughout the Business Analyst hiring process: writing the job posting, reviewing CV and LinkedIn, evaluating the portfolio, preparing a business case, conducting the interview, and making the final decision.

For each candidate, three main questions should be evaluated:

- whether the candidate can turn a vague need into a clear business question;
- whether they understand basic data and KPI logic;
- whether they can communicate with different stakeholders and document agreements.

Recommended decision:

- **Move to the next round** if the candidate asks clarifying questions, frames the problem clearly, and connects analysis with a decision.
- **Needs additional checking** if the candidate communicates well, but the data or KPI logic remains weak.
- **Not suitable at the moment** if the candidate repeats the request without clarifying the goal, metric, or decision.

## Role View and Work

In this group work, we looked at the Business Analyst role from four perspectives. The hiring manager evaluated how the role appears in CV, LinkedIn, and the job posting. The technical interviewer focused on data logic, KPIs, and turning a business question into an analysis task. The HR manager evaluated stakeholder communication. The team lead considered practical daily work: documenting decisions, aligning expectations, and creating recommendations.

We used the same four views in every chapter so that the guide would evaluate business framing, data logic, stakeholder work, and communication together.

## 1. Technical Skills Evaluation

In a Business Analyst role, the candidate does not need to be the strongest programmer, but must understand data logic. Basic SQL, KPI understanding, and the ability to formulate a business question as an analyzable task are expected.

The employer checks whether the candidate asks the right questions: what are we measuring, why are we measuring it, who needs the result, and which decision does it support.

Group result: the technical task could be a business case where the candidate defines the problem, metrics, needed data, and analysis plan.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the candidate clarifies the goal, identifies needed data, defines KPIs, and avoids jumping to conclusions.
- Asked: "What would you ask the stakeholder first?", "Which metric best answers this question?", "How would you know whether the analysis was useful?"

## 2. Portfolio Evaluation

A Business Analyst portfolio should show problem framing, stakeholder needs, data use, and a practical recommendation. It does not need to be the most technical portfolio, but it must show clear thinking.

The employer checks whether the candidate can explain why the analysis was needed and how the result supports a business decision.

Group result: a strong portfolio contains a business problem, analysis logic, stakeholder view, and recommendation.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the project explains the business context, requirements, data, analysis, and recommendation.
- Asked: "What was the original business need?", "How did you turn it into an analysis question?", "Who used the result?"

## 3. CV and LinkedIn Evaluation

The CV should emphasize business understanding, requirements gathering, KPI thinking, stakeholder communication, and data-based decisions. Previous experience in sales, operations, customer service, or project coordination can be valuable.

The LinkedIn headline could be: "Business Analyst | KPI Thinking, SQL Basics, Stakeholder Communication | Data-Driven Decisions".

Group result: CV and LinkedIn should show that the candidate can connect business needs and data work.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the CV includes project examples, business outcomes, stakeholder work, and relevant tools.
- Asked: "Which project best shows your business thinking?", "How does your previous experience help in analysis?", "How do you document stakeholder needs?"

## 4. 30-60-90 Day Onboarding Plan

First 30 days: learn UrbanStyle's business model, stakeholders, KPIs, and current reporting needs.

Days 30-60: support one stakeholder request by defining the question, needed data, and output.

Days 60-90: lead one small analysis or requirements project from problem definition to recommendation.

Group result: onboarding should move from understanding the business to independently framing and supporting decisions.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the plan includes stakeholder mapping, KPI learning, requirements gathering, and one practical output.
- Asked: "Which stakeholders would you meet first?", "How would you clarify an unclear request?", "Which small business problem would you analyze first?"

## 5. Writing a Job Offer

The job posting should emphasize business problem framing, stakeholder communication, KPI thinking, and working with data teams.

Requirements: analytical thinking, Excel/Sheets, basic SQL or data literacy, documentation, and clear communication.

Group result: the posting should attract candidates who can connect business needs with analysis, including career changers with strong domain experience.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the job posting describes real business analyst tasks, stakeholders, tools, and expected mindset.
- Asked: "Does the posting overemphasize tools?", "Are stakeholder responsibilities clear?", "Does it explain what decisions the role supports?"

## 6. Interview Scenario

The interview could include a case: "Management says sales are weaker than expected. How would you clarify the problem and plan the analysis?"

The candidate should ask about period, target, channel, categories, customers, and which decision the analysis should support.

Group result: the Business Analyst interview must evaluate problem framing, communication, and data logic.

**Examples: what is reviewed and asked**

- Reviewed: whether the candidate asks clarifying questions, defines success, identifies needed data, and suggests a practical output.
- Asked: "What is the first question you would ask?", "Which KPI would you use?", "How would you present the result to management?"

**More Detailed Interview Structure**

- 5 minutes: introduction and candidate background.
- 15 minutes: portfolio or project walk-through.
- 15 minutes: business case with unclear stakeholder need.
- 15 minutes: STAR questions about communication, conflict, prioritization, and learning.
- 5 minutes: candidate questions.
- 5 minutes: next steps.

**Interview Evaluation Criteria**

- Problem framing: whether the candidate turns an unclear need into a clear analysis task.
- Data logic: whether they understand KPIs and needed data.
- Communication: whether they can work with business and technical teams.
- Collaboration and learning ability: whether they document agreements and adapt based on feedback.

## Strong vs Weak Candidate

## Evaluation Scale Summary

| Area | 1 - needs development | 2 - suitable level | 3 - strong level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Problem framing | Takes the request too literally | Clarifies goal and metrics | Turns a vague need into a clear analysis task |
| Data logic | Cannot name needed data | Understands KPIs and simple SQL | Connects data with decision and impact |
| Stakeholder work | Does not ask stakeholder needs | Maps basic interests | Aligns different expectations and documents agreements |
| Communication | Explanation remains general | Explains clearly | Can speak with both business and technical teams |

The candidate is a strong recommendation if problem framing and communication are at least level 2 and one of them is level 3.

**Strong candidate:** can turn a vague business problem into a concrete analysis task, asks clarifying questions, and understands stakeholder needs. Their portfolio or previous experience includes an example where they described a business need, gathered requirements, used data, and reached a practical recommendation.

**Weak candidate:** repeats the business request without clarifying it, focuses on general statements, and cannot define the metric, data, or decision. They may communicate pleasantly but do not create a clear analysis path.
